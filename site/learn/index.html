Earth science · 6 min

Plate tectonics and earthquakes

Earth’s rigid outer shell is broken into plates that move a few centimetres a year. Almost all earthquakes happen where plates meet: they pull apart at mid-ocean ridges, grind past each other on transform faults such as the San Andreas, or one dives beneath another at a subduction zone.

Magnitude is logarithmic. Each whole step releases about 32 times more energy, so an M7 releases roughly 1,000 times the energy of an M5:

E₂ / E₁ = 10^(1.5 · (M₂ − M₁))

On the globe, quakes are coloured by depth. At subduction zones the dots get deeper as you move inland, tracing the sinking slab: the Wadati–Benioff zone.

Space · 7 min

Orbits: from the ISS to geostationary

A satellite is always falling; it just moves sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it. Kepler’s third law links the size of an orbit to how long it takes:

T = 2π √(a³ / μ),  μ = 398,600 km³/s²

For the ISS, a ≈ 6,371 + 420 = 6,791 km, which gives T ≈ 92.8 minutes. Push a out to 42,164 km and T becomes one sidereal day (23 h 56 m): the satellite hangs over one spot on the equator. GPS sits between the two, at 20,200 km altitude, orbiting twice a sidereal day.

Global Intelligence Atlas computes every satellite position in your browser with the SGP4 model from two-line element sets (TLEs) published by CelesTrak.

Earth science · 5 min

Day, night and the seasons

The Sun is directly overhead at one point on Earth at any moment: the subsolar point. Its latitude, the solar declination, follows the tilt of Earth’s axis through the year. A good approximation, with d the day of the year:

δ ≈ 23.44° · sin( 360°/365 · (d − 81) )

The terminator (the day/night line) is the circle 90° away from the subsolar point. At the equinoxes it passes through both poles; at the solstices it touches the polar circles, giving midnight sun on one side of the planet and polar night on the other.

Infrastructure · 5 min

Undersea cables and the speed of light

More than 95 % of intercontinental internet traffic travels through fibre-optic cables on the seabed, not through satellites. Light in glass is slowed by the refractive index n ≈ 1.47:

v = c / n ≈ 299,792 / 1.47 ≈ 204,000 km/s

So New York to London (about 5,570 km along the great circle) can never be faster than roughly 27 ms one way, before any routing, queuing or detours along the real cable path. Use the Measure tool to work it out for any two places.

Infrastructure · 5 min

Chokepoints of world trade

Most seaborne trade follows a handful of lanes that funnel through narrow straits and canals. The U.S. Energy Information Administration estimates that about a fifth of the world’s petroleum liquids consumption passes through the Strait of Hormuz. When the Suez Canal or the Bab el-Mandeb is disrupted, ships sail around Africa instead, adding one to two weeks.

Space · 4 min

Aurora and space weather

The solar wind carries the Sun’s magnetic field outward. When it connects with Earth’s field, energy pours into the magnetosphere and electrons rain down along field lines near the poles, lighting oxygen (green, red) and nitrogen (blue, purple) about 100–300 km up.

Geomagnetic activity is summarised by the planetary K-index, from 0 to 9. Kp 5 or more is a geomagnetic storm. NOAA’s OVATION model uses solar-wind measurements from spacecraft at the L1 point, about 1.5 million km sunward, which is why aurora forecasts only look 30 to 90 minutes ahead.

Geography · 4 min

Great circles and map projections

The shortest path between two points on a sphere is an arc of a great circle, a circle whose centre is Earth’s centre. On a flat Mercator map it looks curved and bent toward the pole, which is why flights from Los Angeles to Dubai pass over Greenland.

d = 2R · asin √( sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ sin²(Δλ/2) )

That is the haversine formula Global Intelligence Atlas uses for Measure and for scoring the quiz. The globe avoids projection distortion entirely: Greenland really is smaller than Africa by about 14 times.

Earth observation · 4 min

Reading satellite imagery

The “NASA daily” base map is yesterday’s Earth, stitched from the VIIRS instrument on the Suomi NPP satellite and served by NASA’s Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS). Each day VIIRS sweeps a 3,000 km-wide swath on every pass, covering the whole planet in daylight.

You will see gaps shaped like long diamonds near the equator: the spaces between neighbouring swaths, and the seams where one day’s passes meet. Cloud tops, smoke plumes, dust storms and sea ice show up in true colour.

Aviation · 5 min

How flight tracking works

Most airliners broadcast their position, altitude, speed and callsign about twice a second on 1090 MHz: Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B). Anyone with a small antenna can receive it, and volunteer networks such as adsb.lol and the OpenSky Network pool thousands of receivers into one live picture.

The seatback moving map on a plane uses the same numbers. Its “outside temperature” follows the standard atmosphere, which cools 6.5 °C for every kilometre up to about 11 km:

T ≈ 15 °C − 6.5 °C/km × h  (h ≤ 11 km),   then −56.5 °C

At a cruising altitude of 35,000 ft (10.7 km) that gives about −54 °C, which is why cabin windows have three panes.

Civics · 6 min

Cameras, maps and privacy

Two very different kinds of camera share the Cameras group. Traffic cameras are run by road agencies and publish their pictures openly so drivers can check conditions. Automatic licence-plate readers (ALPR), such as those sold by Flock Safety, photograph passing cars and turn plates into searchable records of where and when a vehicle was seen.

Supporters point to recovered stolen cars and solved cases; critics point to long retention, data sharing between agencies, and the ability to reconstruct a person’s movements. Where the readers stand is public knowledge only because volunteers map them in OpenStreetMap, much of it through the DeFlock project.

Questions worth asking about any camera network: who operates it, how long data is kept, who it is shared with, and what oversight exists.

Civics · 5 min

Reading crime data responsibly

Police departments publish incident reports so the public can see what is being recorded. They are useful, and easy to misread. A report is an incident as recorded by police, not a conviction; many crimes are never reported; and places where many people work, shop or visit always show more reports than quiet suburbs, even when the risk to any one person is lower.

To compare places fairly, analysts divide counts by the population exposed (residents, workers or visitors) and look at trends over months, not single weeks:

rate = reports ÷ population × 100,000

Locations are generalised to the block or street segment to protect victims, and categories differ between cities, so a “theft” in Chicago is not always counted the same way as one in London.

Infrastructure · 5 min

How the internet is wired

The internet is not a cloud; it is buildings and glass. Networks meet in a few hundred colocation data centres, where they plug into each other directly or through an internet exchange. Between continents, traffic rides undersea cables; the last few kilometres come over fibre, copper, cable or radio.

Because light in fibre is limited to about 204,000 km/s, where these buildings are matters. A server 3,000 km away can never answer in less than about 30 ms round trip:

RTT ≥ 2 × distance ÷ 204,000 km/s

That is why clouds build regions near big exchanges, and why the busiest buildings sit where many cables land: Ashburn, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, London, Marseille, Singapore, Tokyo.

Space · 5 min

Why rockets launch east, from the coast

Earth spins eastward, and a launch pad rides along. At the equator that is 465 m/s; at latitude φ it is

v = 465 m/s × cos φ

Cape Canaveral (28.5° N) gets about 408 m/s free; Kourou (5.2° N) about 463 m/s. Reaching low orbit takes roughly 7,800 m/s, so that head start saves real fuel. A pad’s latitude is also the lowest orbital tilt (inclination) it can reach directly, which is why geostationary satellites favour launch sites near the equator.

Most pads face an ocean to the east so that spent stages and any failure fall on water, not towns. Open any launch in Global Intelligence Atlas and choose “Why launch from here?” for the numbers at that pad.

Earth science · 5 min

The planet’s wind machine

The tropics receive far more sunlight than the poles. Warm air rises near the equator, flows poleward high up, sinks around 30° north and south and returns along the surface: the Hadley cells. Earth’s rotation turns those return flows sideways (the Coriolis effect), making the steady easterly trade winds; further poleward the westerlies blow, and near the poles the polar easterlies.

Coriolis acceleration: a = 2 Ω v sin φ,   Ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s

The effect vanishes at the equator (sin 0 = 0), which is why hurricanes almost never form within 5° of it, and it is strongest at the poles. In Global Intelligence Atlas’s wind layer you can see all three belts at once, and any storm spinning inside them.

Physics · 6 min

Fluids: why the atmosphere swirls

Air and water obey the Navier–Stokes equations. The Fluid lab runs a fast, stable approximation of them on your graphics card, the “stable fluids” method: move the velocity field along itself (advection), make it incompressible by solving for pressure, and add back small eddies with vorticity confinement.

∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −∇p/ρ + ν∇²u + f,   ∇·u = 0

On the real Earth a rotating frame adds the Coriolis force, which turns winds to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, which is the reason hurricanes spin opposite ways in each hemisphere.
